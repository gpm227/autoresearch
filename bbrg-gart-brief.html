Deal brief · Board & Brew (BBRG, LLC)

Board & Brew: asset summary and acquisition plan

The asset is a royalty stream and the brand behind it. Three parties own the pieces. The founder wants a succession, cash out plus rollover. The payday comes from growing it over the next five to ten years. Every number and clause below is cited to the seller's FDD (issued February 10, 2026) and its audited financials.

1 · What the asset is

Board & Brew is a 46 year old Southern California sandwich and craft beer brand with 33 restaurants. The purchase is the franchise system: 26 contracts that pay a share of every store's sales, collected every Wednesday, the trademarks behind them, and the development pipeline. 26 restaurants pay today, and zero have left the system in three audited years.

2 · Who owns what, and where the money flows

BBRG, LLC · the franchisor

Owns the revenue right

Holds all 26 franchise agreements: 5% of gross sales on the current form, legacy stores at 4% per their original agreements, plus a 1% brand fund, paid weekly. FY25: $3.0M revenue, $2.61M net income, no debt. Owned 55% by founder Tom Powers, 45% by his partners Bret and Mike.

JJ&N Enterprises · the brand

Owns the trademarks

Holds the Board & Brew marks (federally registered, incontestable) and licenses them to BBRG royalty free. 100% Tom's. Also runs the original 1979 Del Mar restaurant, which stays his. The marks must close together with BBRG or neither is worth the price, which is why every closing is cross conditioned.

The store entities

Seven company stores sit outside BBRG

Owned by the principals' affiliate entities, not by the franchisor, so they do not come along automatically. The two tied to Bret and Mike can come in with their 45% on the same deal structure. Diligence confirms what paper ties each one to the brand.

Ownership percentages for BBRG and JJ&N are per the owners; the FDD does not print either cap table. Both get confirmed against the operating agreement and JJ&N's ledger in the document pull.

3 · A founder led succession · cash out and rollover

Tom Powers started the brand in 1979 and still owns the controlling 55% plus the marks. He sells both for a $20M package: $4M to $5M cash at close, a $6M to $7M note paying roughly $1M a year, and a founder's royalty of $8M to $10M earned as the system grows. He keeps the original Del Mar restaurant, takes an advisory role on separate paper, and his rollover is JJ&N itself: the marks entity converts to holdco equity at closing, tax deferred, with the Del Mar restaurant carved out to him first. His partners' 45% is bought with cash, note, and earnout; their path to holdco equity is the same one every store owner has, growth. The structure also makes Tom the transition's lead salesman: his royalty pays from renewals and growth, so the founder the franchisees have trusted since 1979 is the one asking them to renew under the holdco. His advisory work sits on separate paper, and the earnout ties to results only, which preserves the tax treatment. Full offer terms and the two closing options are below.

4 · Where the payday is · growing it over the next five to ten years

The firm money buys the machine at roughly 4.5x current earnings. The return comes from growth: legacy stores stepping from 4% to 5% as they renew (structural upside, on each contract's own dates), stores bought at prices the market has already validated through the match right, real estate accruing deal by deal, the pre sold development pipeline behind $583K of deposits (obligations we service, openings we collect on), and measured new unit expansion: franchise fee revenue doubled to $121K in FY25, and the holdco adds capital and support to open faster, with the pace set by operations. Tom's earnout pays only out of that same growth, so the founder's payday and Gart's are the same curve. If the system grows, everyone gets paid more. If it does not, Gart owns a debt light royalty machine bought at a conservative price.

Contingencies · pre built answers for what we have not read

If JJ&N is an S corp: Del Mar distributes out to Tom with one layer of tax, then he contributes JJ&N under §721. If it is a C corp: no assets leave the corporation. Either the whole company is contributed with Del Mar inside and leased back to Tom at market rent, or JJ&N stays outside, the license gets repapered perpetual and assignable, the holdco takes a signed purchase option on the marks at a fixed price, and Tom's rollover shifts to a slice of his 55% instead. One tax return decides the route.

If the OA lets the majority appoint and remove managers: Option 1 proceeds as written. If it does not: signed manager resignations and an OA amendment become Close 1 deliverables, and if amendments require unanimity, the deal converts to the five party shape. Either way we know which before anything is priced.

If the license carries a change of control trigger: the JJ&N contribution moots it, since we would own both sides of the license. The trigger only matters if the contribution route fails, and the repaper plus purchase option covers that case.

If legacy FAs renew onto the then current form: step ups land at renewal. If they promise renewal on the same terms: the step waits for final expiry and the earnout span stretches to match, so nobody prepays. If any store holds perpetual fixed terms: it gets valued as a fixed 4% annuity inside the earnout base, so no earnout dollar ever pays for a raise that cannot happen.

If Bret and Mike's stores hold no franchise agreements: they are operating on marks we own with no paper behind them, and the regularization package is ready: current form FAs with a legacy rate rider for a set period, offered as part of their 45% deal.

If the bottom up audit finds the stream lighter than Exhibit B: nothing gets renegotiated. The economic grid prices off verified trailing twelve month royalties by formula, so findings reprice the deal automatically.

If store P&Ls show margins too thin for 5% plus 1%: renewal riders inside a pre authorized band, for example 4 to 4.5 to 5 across two renewals, trade rate for term, and the Creative Fund invests first in the thinnest stores.

If ADA holders claim against the $583K of prepaid development obligations: a holdback carved from Tom's note covers unperformed obligations, with offset rights.

If an unknown liability surfaces after closing: the seller note doubles as indemnity security. Offset rights against the note collateralize the indemnity without an escrow fight.

If the lender funds short: cash at close flexes down and the note flexes up, total price unchanged. Tom's tax preference already points the same direction.

If Tom dies or loses capacity mid process: the letter stage includes a signed option on JJ&N at the formula price and exclusivity that binds his estate, so the marks survive the loss of their most important signature.

If California registration drags: the FDD amendment gets drafted during diligence and filed the day of closing. The blackout pauses new franchise sales only, never royalties, renewals, or transfers.

The capital plan · two options, one destination

1

Option 1. Control first, partners follow on the same terms

Close 1 takes control and the brand for the small check: Tom's 55% plus JJ&N plus the license. One condition: the OA must hand operating control to the 55%. Three people sit as managers today, and manager control gets papered at Close 1 or Close 1 does not happen. From there, weekly royalties pay the paper, the renewal conversation calendar starts immediately, stores get bought at market validated prices when owners choose to sell, and the real estate accrues alongside: leases assigned on transfers, sites that come with store buys, property purchased when it pencils. Bret and Mike come in on the same structure when ready, and a second Gart draw happens only after they have signed. Cheapest dollars, and every seller gets the installment treatment.

And if they never sell, Option 1 still pencils. The second draw simply never happens. The return is 55% of a growing royalty stream, senior returns on the growth capital, and real estate held at the holdco level, where it is 100% ours. Their stake is an option we hold open, priced by formula, with a standing put and call after year five. Estates and time tend to walk through doors like that. The one discipline a permanent minority imposes: every holdco to BBRG transaction stays arm's length and documented, because they will have the information rights to check.

2

Option 2. The fast close: bigger outlay, same destination, sooner

Everyone signs the same day: Tom, Bret and Mike, JJ&N. The check is bigger and earlier, though still mostly lender money, since the audited $2.6M of annual cash flow supports roughly $6M to $8M of senior debt at normal coverage, backed by the LC, with the installment note and earnout carrying the rest. What the extra cash buys is time and certainty: 100% and the marks on day one, one disclosure event, nobody left to wait on. Five signatures invite five negotiations, so the process is built to prevent stalling: one purchase agreement for every seller on identical terms, an omnibus exclusivity signed by all five up front, signatures delivered into escrow as each party finalizes, and a drop dead date after which the offer steps down and then expires. The earnout clocks run from a fixed calendar date, so delay costs the sellers, not us. JJ&N signs the same day as everyone else; there is no version of this closing without the marks.

Time runs on our side, and the process says so quietly. The offer is fair on day one, priced off the same per point formula for every seller and fixed in a one page economic grid all five sign at the letter stage, with the hardest signatures negotiated first and Tom's finalized last, since he is the most motivated seller in the room. The offer does not improve with delay: the committed capital has an expiry, and any later deal reprices off the then current numbers, net of value the holdco has built in the meantime. Bret and Mike have two good outcomes and we accept either: sell the 45% and keep running their stores as franchisees inside a growing system, or sell the stores to us as well. Nobody gets squeezed and nobody gets paid to stall. Wherever the OA lets control close first, the plan starts there without waiting: renewal conversations, measured expansion, and real estate positioning move BBRG toward the succession on their own clock. The omnibus exclusivity carries confidentiality covering franchisee communications until closing, so a longer process cannot leak into the system.

The seller structure · Tom's money arrives tax friendly

1

He sells equity, so the gain is capital, not ordinary

We buy his membership interests in BBRG, LLC. For a partnership taxed LLC that is long term capital gain to him, roughly 23.8% federal at the top instead of ordinary rates. The hot asset carve out is immaterial here since receivables run about $99K. IRC §741 §751

2

The earnout is purchase price on the installment method

Papered as contingent purchase price for the equity, he pays tax as each payment lands, not at signing on money he has not seen. Each payment carries stated interest at the AFR so the IRS does not recharacterize principal into interest for him. IRC §453 §483 / §1274

3

No strings to his paycheck, ever

The earnout must never depend on Tom staying employed. Any service condition converts capital gain into ordinary compensation plus payroll tax and wrecks the structure. Transition consulting is separate paper at a market rate, and the earnout pays whether he shows up or not.

4

The marks money stays clean

If JJ&N sells the marks, it is a fixed price with a complete transfer. No retained control, no contingent royalty stream, because contingent trademark payments are ordinary income to the seller. If JJ&N's corporate status makes an asset sale double taxed, we repaper the license perpetual and assignable instead and skip the sale. IRC §1253

5

The rollover is JJ&N, deferred entirely

Tom contributes the marks entity to the holdco for units instead of selling it for cash. No tax until he sells the units, no cash spent buying the marks, and the brand's founder sits on the holdco cap table for the whole succession. The 55% purchase and the contribution run as separate exchanges to stay clear of the disguised sale rules. Del Mar gets carved out to Tom before the contribution, and JJ&N's corporate tax status sets the carve out mechanics. IRC §721 §707

6

How we pay for all of it

Buying 100% of the LLC interests is treated as an asset purchase for tax on our side. Book equity is about $0.4M, so nearly the entire price steps up and amortizes over 15 years. That deduction stream is real money to us, and it funds the structure Tom wants. His tax friendliness costs us nothing. Rev. Rul. 99-6 IRC §197 §743 / §754

One caveat: Tom lives in Del Mar, and California taxes his gain at ordinary state rates regardless of structure. The installment method still works for California, but nothing here erases state tax. These are deal design points, not tax advice, and the final structure runs through tax counsel before anything is signed.

Structural upside at renewal, priced as upside
