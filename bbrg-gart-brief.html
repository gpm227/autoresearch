Deal brief · Board & Brew (BBRG, LLC)

Board & Brew: asset summary and acquisition plan

The asset is a royalty stream and the brand behind it. Three parties own the pieces. The founder wants a succession, cash out plus rollover. The payday comes from growing it over the next five to ten years. Every number and clause below is cited to the seller's FDD (issued February 10, 2026) and its audited financials.

1 · What the asset is

Board & Brew is a 46 year old Southern California sandwich and craft beer brand with 33 restaurants. The purchase is the franchise system: 26 contracts that pay a share of every store's sales, collected every Wednesday, the trademarks behind them, and the development pipeline. 26 restaurants pay today, and zero have left the system in three audited years.

2 · Who owns what, and where the money flows

BBRG, LLC · the franchisor

Owns the revenue right

Holds all 26 franchise agreements: 5% of gross sales on the current form (older stores at 4%) plus a 1% brand fund, paid weekly. FY25: $3.0M revenue, $2.61M net income, no debt. Owned 55% by founder Tom Powers, 45% by his partners Bret and Mike.

JJ&N Enterprises · the brand

Owns the trademarks

Holds the Board & Brew marks (federally registered, incontestable) and licenses them to BBRG royalty free. 100% Tom's. Also runs the original 1979 Del Mar restaurant, which stays his. The marks must close together with BBRG or neither is worth the price, which is why every closing is cross conditioned.

The store entities

Seven company stores sit outside BBRG

Owned by the principals' affiliate entities, not by the franchisor, so they do not come along automatically. The two tied to Bret and Mike can come in with their 45% on the same deal structure. Diligence confirms what paper ties each one to the brand.

From FY24 to FY25, royalties went from $2.39M to $2.76M and net income from $2.20M to $2.61M. BBRG makes no Item 19 performance representations, so the audits and store level records are the underwriting base. We want it that way. One number we do not promise: part of the royalty base still pays 4%, and the current contract form steps a renewing store to 5%. That is structural upside, but it arrives store by store on each contract's own dates, some of them years out. We underwrite on the checks that arrive today. The audits back this caution: Creative Fund revenue was only $92K against $2.76M of royalties, and 1% of system sales would run several times that, so most stores are clearly not on the current 5% plus 1% form yet. Their old paper is the upside, and also the reason we do not promise its timing.

3 · A founder led succession · cash out and rollover

Tom Powers started the brand in 1979 and still owns the controlling 55% plus the marks. He sells both for a $20M package: $4M to $5M cash at close, a $6M to $7M note paying roughly $1M a year, and a founder's royalty of $8M to $10M earned as the system grows. He keeps the original Del Mar restaurant, takes an advisory role on separate paper, and can roll part of his proceeds into the holdco, keeping him invested through the succession. His partners can sell on the same structure or roll in alongside him. Full offer terms and the two closing options are below.

4 · Where the payday is · growing it over the next five to ten years

The firm money buys the machine at roughly 4.5x current earnings. The return comes from growth: legacy stores stepping from 4% to 5% as they renew (structural upside, on each contract's own dates), stores bought at prices the market has already validated through the match right, real estate accruing deal by deal, and the pre sold development pipeline sitting behind $583K of deposits. Tom's earnout pays only out of that same growth, so the founder's payday and Gart's are the same curve. If the system grows, everyone gets paid more. If it does not, Gart owns a debt light royalty machine bought at a conservative price.

Economics. Tom takes the case plus earnout, with real cash at close

If he still holds out for all cash at $20M and up, his own disclosure numbers do not carry the price.

G4

Structure. The OA picks the closing shape, not the destination

The operating agreement decides one thing: who signs on day one. A drag along pulls Bret and Mike to the same table. Blocked transfers force all five parties to sign the same day. Free transfers mean control closes first and the 45% follows. The franchisee side is already settled, since the FAs put no restriction on our right to assign. The OA does not change the destination. It sets which of the two buying options is open and what the fast one costs.

If there is no drag: JJ&N and Tom's 55% close first, under one exclusivity. Tom runs both entities, so one signature set locks both. Bret and Mike then get the same structure Tom got: cash, note, earnout, and holdco rollover, on their timeline. The holdco plan is built so every existing owner gains from it, which is what actually moves them. Speed on this path comes from the work: the bottom up store level audit, the complete document set, and renewal conversations scheduled early and run as value conversations, since a renewing store under the holdco gets brand fund investment, operating support, and a growth plan its owner shares in. Their two stores stay theirs unless they choose to sell them in; any sale of a store comes to us first under FA §12.3.

JJ&N is confirmed: Tom owns 100% of it. Bret and Mike hold none, so one exclusivity and one signature set locks the 55% and the brand together, and nobody can shop BBRG with the marks attached. We still pull the full JJ&N file, because knowing the owner is not the same as knowing the company: its tax status (C corp or S corp decides whether it sells us the marks or repapers the license), the complete mark and domain schedule, any liens on the IP, any direct licenses JJ&N ever granted outside the franchise system (the 2012 California notice involved exactly one), and the paper on the original Del Mar store it has operated since 1979, which comes along if we buy the entity instead of the marks.

The capital plan · two options, one destination

1

Option 1. Control first, partners follow on the same terms

Close 1 takes control and the brand for the small check: Tom's 55% plus JJ&N plus the license. One condition: the OA must hand operating control to the 55%. Three people sit as managers today, and manager control gets papered at Close 1 or Close 1 does not happen. From there, weekly royalties pay the paper, the renewal conversation calendar starts immediately, stores get bought at market validated prices when owners choose to sell, and the real estate accrues alongside: leases assigned on transfers, sites that come with store buys, property purchased when it pencils. Bret and Mike come in on the same structure when ready, and a second Gart draw happens only after they have signed. Cheapest dollars, and every seller gets the installment and rollover treatment.

2

Option 2. The fast close: bigger outlay, same destination, sooner

Everyone signs the same day: Tom, Bret and Mike, JJ&N. The check is bigger and earlier, though still mostly lender money, since the audited $2.6M of annual cash flow supports roughly $6M to $8M of senior debt at normal coverage, backed by the LC, with the installment note and earnout carrying the rest. What the extra cash buys is time and certainty: 100% and the marks on day one, one disclosure event, nobody left to wait on.

✓

The OA sets the menu. The rule holds under both

A drag along means we choose either option. Blocked transfers mean the fast close is the only lawful shape. Free transfers make the slow build available and cheapest. Under every option, each Gart dollar draws against a cross conditioned closing that cannot leave a partial state. Nothing funds a maybe, and in both options the business largely finances its own purchase out of weekly royalties.

Ranges below are the opening shape, locked at Gate 1 and re cut after the Gate 2 document pull prices the royalty schedule. The letter to Tom · what makes him sign

The headline is his number. The letter leads with $20M to Tom, the figure he anchored on, achieved on his system's performance. Cash at close is one component of it.

The cash is committed. The LC removes the financing contingency: the day one check is funded before the letter goes out. After years of unserious buyers, that is what separates this letter from the rest of his mail.

His income never dips. From the first year, note payments plus earnout meet or beat the roughly $1.4M BBRG pays him today, taxed as capital gain instead of ordinary income. His income steps up at signing rather than waiting on the back end.

The earnout is the founder's royalty. He participates in every renewal and every store added for the span of the succession, paid from the growth itself.

The legacy terms are cheap for us and matter to him. He keeps the original Del Mar operation. A founder title and advisory role on separate, market rate paper. His people, the ops and development team named in the FDD, keep their seats. Written commitment to the brand he named. Optional rollover so he owns a piece of what it becomes.

The letter asks for one thing back: exclusivity covering the 55% and JJ&N together, so the succession we are funding cannot be shopped while we build it.

The seller structure · Tom's money arrives tax friendly

1

He sells equity, so the gain is capital, not ordinary

We buy his membership interests in BBRG, LLC. For a partnership taxed LLC that is long term capital gain to him, roughly 23.8% federal at the top instead of ordinary rates. The hot asset carve out is immaterial here since receivables run about $99K. IRC §741 §751

2

The earnout is purchase price on the installment method

Papered as contingent purchase price for the equity, he pays tax as each payment lands, not at signing on money he has not seen. Each payment carries stated interest at the AFR so the IRS does not recharacterize principal into interest for him. IRC §453 §483 / §1274

3

No strings to his paycheck, ever

The earnout must never depend on Tom staying employed. Any service condition converts capital gain into ordinary compensation plus payroll tax and wrecks the structure. Transition consulting is separate paper at a market rate, and the earnout pays whether he shows up or not.

4

The marks money stays clean

If JJ&N sells the marks, it is a fixed price with a complete transfer. No retained control, no contingent royalty stream, because contingent trademark payments are ordinary income to the seller. If JJ&N's corporate status makes an asset sale double taxed, we repaper the license perpetual and assignable instead and skip the sale. IRC §1253

5

Optional: he rolls a slice and defers it entirely

Tom can roll part of his 55% into the buyer platform tax deferred. No tax on the rolled piece until it is sold, and it keeps him aligned through the earnout years. IRC §721

6

How we pay for all of it

Buying 100% of the LLC interests is treated as an asset purchase for tax on our side. Book equity is about $0.4M, so nearly the entire price steps up and amortizes over 15 years. That deduction stream is real money to us, and it funds the structure Tom wants. His tax friendliness costs us nothing. Rev. Rul. 99-6 IRC §197 §743 / §754

One caveat: Tom lives in Del Mar, and California taxes his gain at ordinary state rates regardless of structure. The installment method still works for California, but nothing here erases state tax. These are deal design points, not tax advice, and the final structure runs through tax counsel before anything is signed.

Then the machine runs · where money comes from, in order

1

Weekly cash from day one

5% of gross revenues plus a 1% Creative Fund (raisable to 2% on 90 days notice), collected every Wednesday, 52 times a year. Items 5 and 6

2

Structural upside at renewal, priced as upside

Renewal requires the then current form, which per the contract "WILL CONTAIN THE ROYALTY … PAYABLE BY NEW BOARD AND BREW FRANCHISEES." So legacy 4% stores step up when they renew or when their paper expires. The mechanism is contractual. The timing is not: each store's own agreement sets its date, some near, some years out, and no rate ever moves mid term. The underwriting does not lean on these raises, and the earnout absorbs the timing risk, because if the raises come slow the earnout pays slower with them. FA §5.2(d) Item 17(c)

3

Turnover upgrades the paper

Every consented store sale can require the buyer to sign the current form, plus a $10,000 transfer fee. FA §12.2(c)(iv)

4

Store buys at market proven prices

Any real offer has to be shown to us in full, and we can take the same deal within 15 days. We acquire at prices a third party already validated. FA §12.3(b) and (c)

5

The real estate stacks up

Every renewal and store purchase is a site decision. Leases get assigned on transfers, locations come with store buys, and property gets bought when it pencils. Over time the annuity funds a real estate position under the brand it hosts.

6

The earnout pays itself

It gets collected from the royalty stream the seller's own audits prove. We fund the provable part, and performance funds the rest. Item 21 / Exhibit B
