Deal brief · Board & Brew (BBRG, LLC)

Board & Brew: asset summary and acquisition plan

Store operations are solid. The corporate layer is not maximizing the brand. The stores sold $21.2M in Q1 2026, up 10.7% comparable and led by traffic, while roughly $700K of overhead runs the entire franchisor, renewal paper sits behind the calendar, and the growth engine is unbuilt. The plan buys the corporate layer and builds it to the standard of the stores it serves; the value created is a royalty base that compounds over the next five to ten years. Three parties own the pieces and the founder wants a succession. Every number and clause below is cited to the seller's FDD, its audited financials, and the Q1 2026 sales report.

1 · What the asset is

Board & Brew is a Southern California sandwich and craft beer brand operating since 1979, with 33 restaurants. The purchase is the franchise system: 26 contracts that pay a share of every store's sales, collected every Wednesday, the trademarks behind them, and the development pipeline. Across three audited years the system lost one franchised outlet to termination and none to non renewal, and two more agreements are signed with stores not yet open. In Q1 2026 the system sold $21.2M, up 15.9% year over year, with 29 of 30 comparable stores growing and average unit volume pacing $2.62M.

2 · Who owns what, and where the money flows

BBRG, LLC · the franchisor

Owns the revenue right

Holds the franchise agreements, 26 per Item 20 and 27 per the audit, reconciled in the file review: 5% of gross sales on the current form, about 4% blended across the system per the Q1 sales data, plus a 1% brand fund, paid weekly. FY25: $3.0M revenue, $2.61M net income, no debt. Owned 55% by founder Tom Powers, 45% by his partners Bret and Mike.

JJ&N Enterprises · the brand

Owns the trademarks

Holds the Board & Brew marks (federally registered; §8 and §15 declaration accepted 2023) and licenses them to BBRG royalty free. 100% Tom's. Also runs the original 1979 Del Mar restaurant, which stays his. The marks close together with BBRG; every closing is cross conditioned.

The store entities

Seven company stores sit outside BBRG

Owned by the principals' affiliate entities; they do not come along automatically. The two tied to Bret and Mike can come in with their 45% on the same deal structure. Diligence confirms what paper ties each one to the brand.

Ownership percentages for BBRG and JJ&N are per the owners; the FDD does not print either cap table. Both get confirmed against the operating agreement and JJ&N's ledger in the document pull.

3 · A founder led succession · cash out and rollover

Tom Powers started the brand in 1979 and still owns the controlling 55% plus the marks. He sells both for a $20M package: $4M to $5M cash at close, a $6M to $7M note paying roughly $1M a year, and a founder's royalty of $8M to $10M earned as the system grows. He keeps the original Del Mar restaurant, takes an advisory role on separate paper, and his rollover is JJ&N itself: the marks entity converts to holdco equity at closing, tax deferred, with the Del Mar restaurant carved out to him first. Del Mar itself, pacing $2.77M a year in the Q1 report, signs the current franchise form at closing and pays the same royalty as every other store, and the holdco holds a right of first refusal and a call at the grid price on the store and any related real estate, so the flagship folds in whenever Tom chooses. His partners' 45% is bought with cash, note, and earnout; their path to holdco equity is the same one every store owner has, growth. His royalty pays from renewals and growth, so Tom makes the renewal case to the franchisees himself. Advisory work sits on separate paper; the earnout ties to results only, preserving the tax treatment.

4 · Value creation · compounding the asset over five to ten years

The firm money is roughly 4.5x current earnings; the full package including the earnout is 7.7x. The stores are already doing their part: the Q1 2026 pace annualizes near $85M of system sales, which at the 4% blend implies royalties near $3.4M against the $2.76M FY25 base before any corporate action. The build converts that store performance into franchisor value through five levers. Legacy stores step from 4% to 5% as each contract renews on its own dates. Stores get bought at prices a third party has already validated through the match right. Real estate accrues deal by deal. The development pipeline behind $586K of deferred fees gets serviced and converted into open stores. New units open faster with holdco capital and support; franchise fee revenue doubled to $121K in FY25. Tom's earnout pays only out of that same growth. If growth stalls, the entry price stands on current earnings alone.

Contingencies · pre built answers for what we have not read

If JJ&N is an S corp: Del Mar distributes out to Tom with one layer of tax, then he contributes JJ&N under §721. If it is a C corp: no assets leave the corporation. Either the whole company is contributed with Del Mar inside and leased back to Tom at market rent, or JJ&N stays outside, the license gets repapered perpetual and assignable, the holdco takes a signed purchase option on the marks at a fixed price, and Tom's rollover shifts to a slice of his 55% instead. One tax return decides the route.

If the OA lets the majority appoint and remove managers, or says nothing: Option 1 proceeds, because California's default rule hands both powers to a majority of the members. If the OA entrenches the managers in writing: their cooperation cannot be bought from Tom alone, so Option 1 comes off the menu and the deal is the five party shape or nothing. The read still checks member reserved matters, the amendment threshold, and the dissolution vote, which set the price of their veto. With a drag or with majority manager rights, there are reasonable paths. With neither, the deal happens only if all five want it to.

If Bret and Mike, once approached, say no and go shopping, or move to buy Tom out themselves: Tom's exclusivity bars any sale, including to other members, for the window, and the JJ&N option outlives it. If the license carries change of control or assignment limits, an insider buyout loses the brand. If the license reads as strong as Item 13 summarizes, it protects any BBRG owner, and the defenses are an exclusivity long enough to close inside plus a package an insider cash bid cannot match after tax. The option then becomes a position a counter bidder must buy out. Nothing flips inside the window.

If the license carries a change of control trigger: the JJ&N contribution moots it, since we would own both sides of the license. The trigger only matters if the contribution route fails, and the repaper plus purchase option covers that case.

If legacy FAs renew onto the then current form: step ups land at renewal. If they promise renewal on the same terms: the step waits for final expiry and the earnout span stretches to match, so nobody prepays. If any store holds perpetual fixed terms: it gets valued as a fixed 4% annuity inside the earnout base, so no earnout dollar ever pays for a raise that cannot happen.

If affiliate outlets contribute to the Creative Fund but pay no royalty (the computed 4% blend against total system sales leaves several million dollars of sales paying fund contributions but no royalty): current form FAs for every affiliate outlet outside the deal are a closing deliverable, executed at signing by the same people selling the company. At the system average that is several hundred thousand dollars a year of royalty, negotiated inside the deal rather than with independent operators.

If executed agreements confirm stores at or past expiration (the store schedule computes several, concentrated with the largest operator): a standstill covenant runs from the letter date, no renewal, addendum, transfer consent, or rate concession without buyer consent. Re papering those stores onto the current form is a closing condition; any store not papered at closing adjusts price by formula or carries a specific indemnity. Tom's founder's royalty carries the same gate: its first tranche pays only after that group signs.

If the largest operator declines to renew and de brands: California will not enforce the post term covenant, so the case gets priced rather than argued. The grid values that bloc's royalties at zero until papered, and the deal stands on the remainder or walks. This is why the expired agreements read in week two alongside the OA and the license.

If the bottom up audit finds the stream lighter than Exhibit B: nothing gets renegotiated. The economic grid prices off verified trailing twelve month royalties by formula, with rate mix a named input, so findings reprice the deal automatically.

If store P&Ls show margins too thin for 5% plus 1%: renewal riders inside a pre authorized band, for example 4 to 4.5 to 5 across two renewals, trade rate for term, and the Creative Fund invests first in the thinnest stores.

If holders claim against the $586K of deferred fee obligations: a holdback carved from Tom's note covers unperformed obligations, with offset rights.

If an unknown liability surfaces after closing: the seller note doubles as indemnity security. Offset rights against the note collateralize the indemnity without an escrow fight.

If the lender funds short: cash at close flexes down and the note flexes up, total price unchanged. Tom's tax preference already points the same direction.

If Tom dies or loses capacity mid process: the letter stage includes a signed option on JJ&N at the formula price and exclusivity that binds his estate, so the marks survive the loss of their most important signature.

If California registration drags: the FDD amendment gets drafted during diligence and filed the day of closing. The blackout pauses new franchise sales only, never royalties, renewals, or transfers.

Close 1 takes control and the brand for the small check: Tom's 55% plus JJ&N plus the license. One condition: the OA must hand operating control to the 55%. If they never sell, Option 1 still pencils: the second draw never happens, and the return is 55% of a growing royalty stream, senior returns on growth capital, and real estate held at the holdco. Their stake stays an open option, priced by formula, with a standing put and call after year five. Every holdco to BBRG transaction stays arm's length and documented; they keep information rights.

2

Option 2. The fast close: bigger outlay, same destination, sooner

Everyone signs the same day: Tom, Bret and Mike, JJ&N. The check is bigger and earlier. Adjusted cash flow, net of the management fee and the related party rebate, runs about $1.9M and supports roughly $3M to $4M of senior debt at 1.35 to 1.6x coverage, backed by the LC. Gart equity bridges the balance of the day one check; the installment note and earnout carry the rest. Lower leverage is the better deal anyway, since the platform build is the return. The extra cash buys time and certainty: 100% and the marks day one, one disclosure event. Five signatures invite five negotiations, so: one purchase agreement for every seller on identical terms, an omnibus exclusivity signed by all five up front, signatures delivered into escrow as each party finalizes, and a drop dead date after which the offer steps down and then expires. The earnout clocks run from a fixed calendar date, so delay costs the sellers, not us. JJ&N signs the same day; there is no closing without the marks.

The offer is fair on day one, priced off the same per point formula for every seller and fixed in a one page economic grid all five sign at the letter stage, with the hardest signatures negotiated first and Tom's finalized last. The offer does not improve with delay: the committed capital has an expiry, and any later deal reprices off the then current numbers, net of value the holdco has built in the meantime. Bret and Mike can sell the 45% and keep running their stores, or sell the stores as well. Wherever control closes first, the plan starts without waiting. The omnibus exclusivity carries confidentiality covering franchisee communications until closing, so a longer process cannot leak into the system.

The seller structure · how Tom is taxed

1

He sells membership interests, taxed as capital gain

We buy his membership interests in BBRG, LLC. For a partnership taxed LLC that is long term capital gain to him, roughly 23.8% federal at the top instead of ordinary rates. The hot asset carve out is small: receivables run about $149K. IRC §741 §751

2

The earnout is purchase price on the installment method

Papered as contingent purchase price for the equity, he pays tax as each payment lands. Each payment carries stated interest at the AFR so the IRS does not recharacterize principal into interest for him. IRC §453 §483 / §1274

3

No employment conditions on the earnout

The earnout must never depend on Tom staying employed. Any service condition converts capital gain into ordinary compensation plus payroll tax. Transition consulting is separate paper at a market rate, and the earnout pays whether he shows up or not.

4

Any marks sale is a fixed price, complete transfer

If JJ&N sells the marks, it is a fixed price with a complete transfer. No retained control, no contingent royalty stream, because contingent trademark payments are ordinary income to the seller. If JJ&N's corporate status makes an asset sale double taxed, we repaper the license perpetual and assignable instead and skip the sale. IRC §1253

5

The rollover is the JJ&N contribution, tax deferred

Tom contributes the marks entity to the holdco for units instead of selling it for cash. No tax until he sells the units, no cash spent buying the marks, and the brand's founder sits on the holdco cap table for the whole succession. The 55% purchase and the contribution run as separate exchanges to stay clear of the disguised sale rules. Del Mar gets carved out to Tom before the contribution, and JJ&N's corporate tax status sets the carve out mechanics. IRC §721 §707

6

How we pay for all of it

Buying 100% of the LLC interests is treated as an asset purchase for tax on our side. Book equity is about $0.5M, so nearly the entire price steps up and amortizes over 15 years. That deduction stream funds the structure Tom wants. Rev. Rul. 99-6 IRC §197 §743 / §754

One caveat: Tom lives in Del Mar, and California taxes his gain at ordinary state rates regardless of structure. The installment method still works for California, but nothing here erases state tax. These are deal design points rather than tax advice, and the final structure runs through tax counsel before anything is signed.
