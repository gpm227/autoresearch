Deal brief · Board & Brew (BBRG, LLC)

One path. Four gates. No dead ends.

Every closing is cross conditioned. Control, the marks, and the minority endgame fund together or nothing funds. There is no version of this deal where we hold one piece and negotiate for the rest. Every number and clause in this brief comes straight out of the seller's FDD (issued February 10, 2026) and the audited financials attached to it.

The deal in plain words

What this is. Board & Brew is a Southern California sandwich and craft beer brand with 33 locations. 26 belong to franchisees who pay the parent company a cut of sales every week. Last year that produced about $2.8M in royalties and $2.6M in profit on a tiny cost base, with audited books and zero franchisee departures in three years.

What we buy. The whole machine, in three pieces: Tom Powers' controlling 55% of BBRG, the brand name itself (held by Tom's separate company JJ&N), and eventually the partners' 45% (Bret and Mike). We never hold one piece without the others locked, because each piece alone is worth far less than the bundle.

Where the upside sits. New stores pay 5% of sales. Some older stores pay 4%, and the current contract form steps a store up to the new terms when it renews. That upside is written into signed contracts, but it lands store by store on each contract's own schedule, and some of those dates are years away. We price the deal on today's payments and treat the raises as upside, not as the plan.

Two ways to buy it. Slow: control and the brand close first on a small check, then the franchise annuity, the tax friendly installment paper, store purchases, and real estate accumulate as contracts renew and stores change hands, until the partners' 45% comes in. Fast: everyone signs the same day, which takes a bigger cash outlay sooner and simply gets us there faster. Same destination either way, and Tom's preference for being paid over years keeps the up front number small in both.

If the partners won't sell. Nothing forces them, and we don't pretend otherwise. After we own control and the brand, their 45% pays them nothing, nobody else wants it, and their own restaurants renew through us. Then we make a fair offer with the same tax treatment Tom got. They sell because holding is worse.

What we refuse · the dead ends

Dead end 1

Marks without the machine

JJ&N's brand in hand but no BBRG. A logo with zero royalty contracts and nothing to collect. We never buy the marks alone.

Dead end 2

Machine without the marks

BBRG without the license locked up. That is $2.76M of royalties dying store by store as each agreement expires. Item 13 says franchisees keep the marks only through their current terms. A melting annuity.

Dead end 3

Control without an endgame

55% plus a hostile 45% and no contractual way to finish. Deadlock, and every dispute shows up in our own Item 3 where every prospect reads it. Control has to come with the mechanics that finish the job.

The one path · four gates, then one closing

G1

Economics. Tom takes the case plus earnout, with real cash at close

Price anchors to the audited stream. Cash for what Exhibit B proves, earnout for the upside as it actually arrives. The earnout gets paid out of the same weekly royalties and sits junior to LC repayment, and the whole package is built tax friendly for Tom (see the seller structure below). Nobody prepays for upside.

Tom's number. He takes home about $4M a year today, so the check at close has to be real, and we should say so. But the $4M splits. Only about $1.4M of it is BBRG: his 55% of the roughly $2.5M the members distribute each year, assuming distributions run pro rata. The rest is his stores and the Del Mar operation, which he keeps. So the deal replaces about $1.4M of annual income and buys his asset. It does not have to replace his whole living. Between the day one check, note payments with AFR interest, and the earnout, his annual receipts stay near what he makes now, taxed as capital gain instead of ordinary income. If he still holds out for all cash at $20M and up, his own disclosure numbers do not carry the price.

G3

Marks. Secured in writing or nothing closes

Either JJ&N sells the marks into the deal, or the license gets repapered perpetual, change of control proof, and assignable. The disclosed license is already strong. G4

Structure. The OA picks the closing shape, not the destination

The operating agreement decides one thing: who signs on day one. A drag along pulls Bret and Mike to the same table. Blocked transfers force all five parties to sign the same day. Free transfers mean control closes first and the 45% follows. The franchisee side is already settled, since the FAs put no restriction on our right to assign. The OA does not change the destination. It sets which of the two buying options is open and what the fast one costs.

If there is no drag, we do not pretend anything forces the minority. The sequencing does the work instead. JJ&N and Tom's 55% close first, under one exclusivity, before Bret and Mike ever hear a number. Tom runs both entities, so one signature set locks both. After that closing, holding the 45% means: no rational third party will buy it, since the marks and control are gone. Distributions can lawfully shift from payouts to growth and debt service, so the stake stops yielding. And their stores need us either way: if their two stores hold franchise agreements, renewals and any sale run through us; if they instead sit among the seven affiliate outlets with no franchise agreements, then they are using our marks with no license at all, and they need paper from us the day after closing. The document pull tells us which lever we hold. Then the blended offer arrives, priced fairly, with the same installment and rollover tax structure Tom got. Nobody is squeezed. Holding is just the worst seat at the table.

JJ&N is confirmed: Tom owns 100% of it. Bret and Mike hold none, so one exclusivity and one signature set locks the 55% and the brand together, and nobody can shop BBRG with the marks attached. We still pull the full JJ&N file, because knowing the owner is not the same as knowing the company: its tax status (C corp or S corp decides whether it sells us the marks or repapers the license), the complete mark and domain schedule, any liens on the IP, any direct licenses JJ&N ever granted outside the franchise system (the 2012 California notice involved exactly one), and the paper on the original Del Mar store it has operated since 1979, which comes along if we buy the entity instead of the marks.

The capital plan · two options, one destination

1

Option 1. The slow build: small checks while the system transitions into our hands

Close 1 takes control and the brand for the small check: Tom's 55% plus JJ&N plus the license. One condition before anything else: the OA must hand operating control to the 55%. Three people sit as managers today, and if the OA does not let the majority member appoint and remove managers, Close 1 buys economics without the steering wheel. Manager control gets papered at Close 1 or Close 1 does not happen. From there the machine does the work. Weekly royalties pay the paper. Stores get bought at matched prices as real offers surface. Renewals arrive on their own dates. And the real estate accrues alongside: leases assigned on transfers, sites that come with store buys, property purchased when it pencils. The 45% gets swept when holding stops paying, and a second Gart draw happens only after Bret and Mike have signed. Slowest calendar, cheapest dollars, and the sellers get the installment and rollover treatment they want.

2

Option 2. The fast close: bigger outlay, same destination, sooner

Everyone signs the same day: Tom, Bret and Mike, JJ&N. The check is bigger and earlier, though still mostly lender money, since the audited $2.6M of annual cash flow supports roughly $6M to $8M of senior debt at normal coverage, backed by the LC, with the installment note and earnout carrying the rest. What the extra cash buys is time and certainty: 100% and the marks on day one, one disclosure event, nobody left to wait on.

✓

The OA sets the menu. The rule holds under both

A drag along means we choose either option. Blocked transfers mean the fast close is the only lawful shape. Free transfers make the slow build available and cheapest. Under every option, each Gart dollar draws against a cross conditioned closing that cannot leave a partial state. Nothing funds a maybe, and in both options the business largely finances its own purchase out of weekly royalties.

The letter to Tom · juicy where it counts

The headline is the whole number. The letter leads with total consideration for the entire platform, the number that values what he built since 1979. Cash at close is a component inside it, never the headline.

The cash is certain, not conditional. The LC makes the day one check a fact rather than a hope. No financing contingency, no broker maybe. For a founder fielding tire kickers, certainty is the juiciest word in the letter.

His income never dips. From the first year, note payments plus earnout meet or beat the roughly $1.4M BBRG pays him today, taxed as capital gain instead of ordinary income. He gets richer the day he signs, not poorer until some earnout someday.

The earnout is the founder's royalty. Framed as his participation in every renewal, every store added, every year the system he seeded keeps compounding over the next several years. He is not being bought out of Board & Brew. He is being paid to complete its succession.

The legacy terms cost us nearly nothing and mean everything. He keeps the original Del Mar operation. A founder title and advisory role on separate, market rate paper. His people, the ops and development team named in the FDD, keep their seats. Written commitment to the brand he named. Optional rollover so he owns a piece of what it becomes.

The letter asks for one thing back: exclusivity covering the 55% and JJ&N together, so the succession we are funding cannot be shopped while we build it.

The seller structure · Tom's money arrives tax friendly

1

He sells equity, so the gain is capital, not ordinary

We buy his membership interests in BBRG, LLC. For a partnership taxed LLC that is long term capital gain to him, roughly 23.8% federal at the top instead of ordinary rates. The hot asset carve out is immaterial here since receivables run about $99K. IRC §741 §751

2

The earnout is purchase price on the installment method

Papered as contingent purchase price for the equity, he pays tax as each payment lands, not at signing on money he has not seen. Each payment carries stated interest at the AFR so the IRS does not recharacterize principal into interest for him. IRC §453 §483 / §1274

3

No strings to his paycheck, ever

The earnout must never depend on Tom staying employed. Any service condition converts capital gain into ordinary compensation plus payroll tax and wrecks the structure. Transition consulting is separate paper at a market rate, and the earnout pays whether he shows up or not.

4

The marks money stays clean

If JJ&N sells the marks, it is a fixed price with a complete transfer. No retained control, no contingent royalty stream, because contingent trademark payments are ordinary income to the seller. If JJ&N's corporate status makes an asset sale double taxed, we repaper the license perpetual and assignable instead and skip the sale. IRC §1253

5

Optional: he rolls a slice and defers it entirely

Tom can roll part of his 55% into the buyer platform tax deferred. No tax on the rolled piece until it is sold, and it keeps him aligned through the earnout years. IRC §721

6

How we pay for all of it

Buying 100% of the LLC interests is treated as an asset purchase for tax on our side. Book equity is about $0.4M, so nearly the entire price steps up and amortizes over 15 years. That deduction stream is real money to us, and it funds the structure Tom wants. His tax friendliness costs us nothing. Rev. Rul. 99-6 IRC §197 §743 / §754

One honest note for the room: Tom lives in Del Mar, and California taxes his gain at ordinary state rates regardless of structure. The installment method still works for California, but nothing here erases state tax. These are deal design points, not tax advice, and the final structure runs through tax counsel before anything is signed.

Then the machine runs · where money comes from, in order

1

Weekly cash from day one

5% of gross revenues plus a 1% Creative Fund (raisable to 2% on 90 days notice), collected every Wednesday. 52 paydays a year. Items 5 and 6

2

Structural upside at renewal, priced as upside

Renewal requires the then current form, which per the contract "WILL CONTAIN THE ROYALTY … PAYABLE BY NEW BOARD AND BREW FRANCHISEES." So legacy 4% stores step up when they renew or when their paper expires. The mechanism is contractual. The timing is not: each store's own agreement sets its date, some near, some years out, and no rate ever moves mid term. The underwriting does not lean on these raises, and the earnout absorbs the timing risk, because if the raises come slow the earnout pays slower with them. FA §5.2(d) Item 17(c)

3

Turnover upgrades the paper

Every consented store sale can require the buyer to sign the current form, plus a $10,000 transfer fee. FA §12.2(c)(iv)

4

Store buys at market proven prices

Any real offer has to be shown to us in full, and we can take the same deal within 15 days. We acquire at prices a third party already validated. FA §12.3(b) and (c)

5

The real estate stacks up

Every renewal and store purchase is a site decision. Leases get assigned on transfers, locations come with store buys, and property gets bought when it pencils. Over time the annuity funds a real estate position under the brand it hosts.

6

The earnout pays itself

It gets collected from the royalty stream the seller's own audits prove. We fund the provable part, and performance funds the rest. Item 21 / Exhibit B
