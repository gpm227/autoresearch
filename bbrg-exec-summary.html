Executive summary · Board & Brew (BBRG, LLC)

A proven operating platform, underwritten by an annuity like royalty stream

This is not the purchase of a franchise company. It is control of a 46 year old Southern California restaurant brand whose 33 locations sold $21.2M in the first quarter of 2026, up 10.7% comparable and led by traffic, with average unit volumes of $2.62M. Beneath it sits a contracted royalty stream that requires no capital, carries no debt, and produces $2.6M of net income a year. That stream pays for the transaction and funds the build. The equity is created above it, in company operated restaurants and the real estate under them.

Three pillars

Pillar one · the cash shield

The franchise base is a zero capital cash engine

Twenty six franchise agreements pay roughly 5% of gross sales, collected every Wednesday, against about $700K of total operating expense. FY25: $3.0M of revenue, $2.61M of net income, $2.6M of operating cash flow, no debt. About 89 cents of every incremental royalty dollar reaches operating income.

That stream services acquisition debt, carries corporate overhead, and funds expansion out of operations. Most restaurant rollouts fail because they have no base cash flow and must raise equity for every unit. This one starts with $2.6M a year of it, and the stores generating it are already growing at double digits without us.

Pillar two · the equity compounder

Company operated stores capture the whole economics

A franchised store at the system average pays about $131K of royalty a year. The same store operated by the platform produces roughly $470K of store level EBITDA. Four times the cash per unit, on about $1.1M of build out, a yield on cost in the mid thirties.

This is where enterprise value is created rather than collected. Royalty income is worth a franchisor multiple; store EBITDA is worth a restaurant multiple on a much larger number, and the two together compound faster than either alone. Growth is funded by the cash shield instead of by dilution.

Pillar three · hard asset backing

Owning the real estate under the flagships

The franchisor today owns nothing. A platform that acquires the property under its own restaurants earns rent alongside royalty, finances it against long leases, depreciates it against operating income, and controls the locations permanently.

It also changes what the investment is. Instead of paper claims on other people's businesses, the balance sheet carries appraisable property, financeable at attractive rates, producing distributions sheltered by depreciation. A platform holding royalty, operations, and real estate sells into a materially different buyer pool than an asset light franchisor.

Sequencing · prove it where the brand is strongest, then export

The brand's power is concentrated in Southern California, where volumes run $2.6M and above and the name does the marketing. The single existing out of market location runs roughly 30% below the system average, which is one data point and the only one there is. The disciplined path builds the first company operated units and the first property acquisitions inside the proven trade area, on sites the platform already knows through its own franchisees, then exports the model to markets where Gart brings real estate relationships and operating presence. Out of market expansion is phase two and is capitalized separately, on results rather than on enthusiasm.

The founder's original restaurant is where the model gets proven publicly. The platform acquires the property at appraised value; the founder and his son keep the operating business under a current form franchise agreement and a market rate lease. He converts trapped equity into platform units, keeps the restaurant his family built, and his son runs it. Every other franchisee then sees a template rather than a proposal: sell the building, keep the business. For an operator whose net worth sits in a retail pad, that is liquidity without loss of control, from a landlord whose only interest is the store succeeding. The same structure removes the largest barrier to new units, because a platform that takes the site and subleases to the operator lets good operators open without buying land.

The transaction

1

The founder is paid $20M, structured

$4M to $5M wired at closing, a $6M to $7M installment note, and an $8M to $10M founder's royalty paid from growth above the FY25 base. He rolls the trademark entity into platform equity tax deferred and stays as an advisor through the succession.

2

Two findings decide the deal

The operating agreement must put control with the founder's 55%, by drag along or majority manager rights. The largest franchisee, holding 11 of the 26 agreements and roughly half the royalty line, must sign current form agreements. Both are answered in week two, before heavy spending.

3

Nothing funds partially

Control, the trademarks, and the partners close cross conditioned. The credit line is never exposed to a half completed structure. If a gate fails, the loss is the diligence budget spent to that point, $150K to $700K.

Capital, in stages

Diligence is $900K to $1.4M across 90 to 120 days, staged behind the week two read. The acquisition is equity plus a modest senior facility against $1.9M of adjusted cash flow, with seller paper carrying the balance and the royalty stream servicing it. The build is authorized separately and only on proof: company operated units at roughly $1.1M each, property acquisitions sized after a public records review of all 33 sites establishes which are reachable. No capital for phase two commits until phase one performs, and every dollar deployed is measured against the cash the shield already produces.
